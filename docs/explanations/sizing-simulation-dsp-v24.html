1 · The method you asked for

Your rule: within the $0.07 – $0.45 dip band, split the share budget across every 1¢ level so the 7¢ end is the fattest clip and 45¢ the thinnest, rising linearly as price falls. That is one shape with two free choices — the steepness (how much fatter 7¢ is than 45¢) and the overall scale. I tested steepness 2×, 4×, 8× and scaled 4× to the same average dollar-per-clip the bot uses today ($4.50), so it is not simply “trade bigger”.

But the fat end is not where the bot trades. Of the dip fills in the sample, landed at 38–45¢ — the thinnest part of your curve — and only at 7–15¢, where it is fattest. The shape mostly moves size around a zone the bot barely visits.

5 · Why the shape is not the lever

Two numbers explain almost everything. Locked capture is ~1.2–1.8¢ per paired share in every configuration (the hedge follows the dip, so the pair cost sits at 0.982–0.989 no matter how you size). A share left naked at expiry costs ~20–40¢ — roughly 20× what a pair earns. So P&L is dominated by how many shares are still unmatched when the window closes, not by how cleverly you sized the entry. In this sample the separator is perfect: all flat windows made money, all stranded windows lost money — in every single arm.

The contradiction in the proposal. The gap veto counts shares, but the risk is in dollars. If you bid 33 shares at 7¢, the veto sees “33 ≥ 11” and blocks it — so with the current knob your curve is mostly refused, and the bot trades on of today's capital ( deployed vs , net ). Free the knob and the curve can finally trade — but now the bot can hold 40+ naked shares, and the worst window goes from −$4.50 to .

The coherent version is to cap unhedged dollars instead: at 7¢ you may take the full 33-share clip ($2.31 of risk), at 40¢ only 11 shares ($4.40). That is the “+$5 cap” rows — the only configurations that improved net P&L while using less capital than the baseline. Which one wins changed when the sample grew (§3), so treat the row as a hypothesis.

6 · Two attacks that failed

Two ideas were supposed to rescue this strategy. Both were run on the same 47 windows.

The 2¢ lock: refusing thin pairs doubles the damage

The leak is real — the bot locks 1.38¢ per paired share while a share left naked at expiry costs ~30¢. So: refuse any pair worth less than 2¢. Every pairing gate moves to 0.980 (fresh, close and rescue).

The capture per pair more than doubles — and the strategy gets worse. Refusing a thin hedge does not remove the position, it just leaves the dip unhedged: naked shares at expiry go from −$55 to −$119. A 1.38¢ lock is a bad deal that still beats a 30¢ loss. The 2¢ lock turns a slow bleed into a coin flip (18 wins, but the best window jumps to +$8.93).

Queue realism: the fill assumption is doing the work

Every arm above assumes a resting maker order fills whenever the engine decides to place it. In the recorded tape there are only top-of-book quotes — no trade prints, no queue position. The strict arm only counts a fill when the opposite side's ask reaches our level, i.e. the price demonstrably traded through us.

78% of the dip fills in the recorded run cannot be verified from the tape. Every number in this report — including the −$2.23 for v2.5 — rests on those fills being real. The strict arm is a lower bound, not a forecast (a taker hitting a resting bid leaves the ask untouched, so real fills sit between the two). But it does mean the sizing question is currently under-determined: no clip curve can be validated against a fill model nobody has checked.

What would settle it: trade prints or depth snapshots for the same windows, so a fill can be confirmed instead of assumed. Until then, the honest summary of §2 is “fewer, smaller positions lose less” — not “this curve is better”.

9 · Method and what this cannot tell you

  • Replay, not re-simulation. Every logged evaluation (quotes for both sides) was fed back through bot/executor.evaluate_microstructure_ladder with the same gates, pacing, sweep phase, cutoff and rescue caps. Only the fresh-dip clip size was swapped; deficit-closing fills still snap to the deficit. The baseline arm reproduces all recorded windows fill-for-fill and dollar-for-dollar — that is the harness' proof.
  • Excluded: (the live loop decided fills the window file never recorded — a process restart, not a strategy difference), plus the final in-flight window.
  • Paper fill model. Fills are assumed at the touch, with unlimited depth and no queue. A 33-share clip at 7¢ is not proven to fill in a real book; the “more volume” arms are therefore optimistic, and the capped arms less so. Every dollar here is conditional on that.
  • n = windows (≈ hours). No arm clears a paired t of 2, and §3 shows the ranking itself moves as windows are added. Re-run on 200+ windows before changing live sizing.
  • Same winner assumption. The market resolved the same way regardless of our orders; in reality a 45-share clip would move a thin book.
